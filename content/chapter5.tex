\chapter{What really is an exponential function?}

\section{Introduction}

\lettrine{Y}{ou} know exponentials, right? You started with powers and roots, and then eventually you extended them to real and complex numbers. And you did that through some really neat properties. You learned that when if you know how to calculate $e^x$ and $e^y$, you can calculate their product easily:
\begin{equation}
    e^{x}e^{y} = e^{x + y}.
\end{equation}
Simple rule. And if you remember your earliest brushes with that function, that was the property your calculus teacher used to define how we extend powers from rational to real numbers in the first place! And it works for complex numbers too.

Now imagine you're studying systems that evolve in time or describing rotations in space. These are no longer real or complex numbers. They're matrices, or more generally, operators. You'd like to use everything you know about exponentials to solve these problems. But here things break down. The familiar rules of exponentials no longer hold. For example, if $A$ and $B$ are matrices, it's generally not true that
\begin{equation}
    AB = BA
\end{equation}
So... what else breaks? These questions will lead us to discover elegant and useful structures. In this chapter, we'll build up the theory of exponentials from scratch, in a way that will make it work for more than numbers.

\section{Exponentials in $\mathbb{R}$: a quick refresher}

Before we go anywhere abstract, let's make sure we understand what we're actually trying to generalize. So let's slow down and remind ourselves how exponential functions work in the real numbers --- the comfortable, familiar world from your calculus class. This isn't just a warm-up. We're going to extract a key idea from this story that will tell us exactly what to do when we leave $\mathbb{R}$ behind.

\subsection{Powers, roots, and rational exponents}

You know powers. For a real number $x$, we can multiply it by itself many times:
\begin{eqnarray*}
    x \cdot x &=& x^2 \\
    x \cdot x \cdot x &=& x^3 \\
    & \vdots & \\
  \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}} &=& x^n
\end{eqnarray*}
Since multiplication of real numbers is associative, we can group these products any way we like. That gives us the two fundamental rules:
\begin{eqnarray}
    x^n \cdot x^m &=& x^{n+m} \label{eq:real_exponent_addition} \\
    (x^n)^m &=& x^{nm} \label{eq:real_exponent_power}
\end{eqnarray}

We can also take roots. The $n$-th root $\sqrt[n]{x}$ is the number whose $n$-th power is $x$. Using \eqref{eq:real_exponent_power}, this is the same as asking: what is $m$ such that $(x^m)^n = x^1$? That means $mn = 1$, so $m = 1/n$. So we can write $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{1/n}$, and more generally:
\begin{equation}
    x^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{x^m}.
\end{equation}
This extends our exponents from integers to rationals.

To get all of $\mathbb{R}$, the standard calculus move is to use limits. Any real number is the limit of a sequence of rationals, so we define:
\begin{equation}
    \text{If } r = \lim_{n \to \infty} r_n \text{ with } r_n \in \mathbb{Q}, \text{ then } x^r := \lim_{n \to \infty} x^{r_n}. \label{eq:real_exp_limit}
\end{equation}
So if we fix the base $r \in \mathbb{R}$, we get a family of functions $\exp_r: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $\exp_r(x) = r^x$. These are the \emph{exponential functions}.

\subsection{The functional equation and a slicker definition}

Now here's a different way to think about what exponential functions \emph{do} --- one that will turn out to be much more useful. Notice the key property they satisfy:
\begin{equation}
    \exp_r(s) \cdot \exp_r(t) = \exp_r(s + t). \label{eq:functional_equation}
\end{equation}
This is just the rule $r^s \cdot r^t = r^{s+t}$. Let's write $f(t) = \exp_r(t)$ and squeeze a differential equation out of this.

Equation \eqref{eq:functional_equation} says $f(s) \cdot f(t) = f(s+t)$. Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$ and then set $t = 0$. Calling $\lambda = f'(0)$:
\begin{equation}
    f'(s) = \lambda \cdot f(s), \quad f(0) = 1.
\end{equation}
This is a simple first-order ODE, and its unique solution is $f(t) = e^{\lambda t}$ where $\lambda = \ln r$. So the functional equation, together with differentiability, forces every exponential to look like $e^{\lambda t}$ for some constant $\lambda$.

But we can also build the solution to $f'(t) = \lambda f(t)$ directly as a power series. Plug $f(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n t^n$ into the ODE and match coefficients, and you get:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:real_exp_series}
    f(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!} = 1 + \lambda t + \frac{(\lambda t)^2}{2!} + \frac{(\lambda t)^3}{3!} + \cdots
\end{equation}
This power series defines $e^{\lambda t}$ using nothing but addition, multiplication, and the ordinary arithmetic of the coefficients $1/n!$. No limits of rational sequences. No roots. No topology. Just sums and products.

That's the clue we needed.

\section{Building exponentials from scratch}

Now let's go abstract. We saw that the power series definition of the exponential only needs addition and multiplication. So let's ask: what are the minimal conditions an algebra needs to satisfy for that definition to make sense?

Let's say we have some set of objects $\mathcal{A} = \{x, y, z, \ldots\}$. At this point we're not worried what they are. We'll could call them ``numbers'', but they could be more complicated objects as well. Let's say also that we know how to do two things with those objects: we can add them $x + y$ and we can multiply them $x \cdot y$.

Outside of those two operations, we'll keep the set $\mathcal{A}$ as abstract as we can. We could choose a specific set of concrete mathematical objects: matrices, functions in a certain domain, quaternions, \ldots, but we won't. And the reason is simple: whatever conclusions we reach by requiring just knowing how to add and multiply things will hold for any concrete set in which we know how to add and multiply things. That's the power of abstract math: if we prove that something holds given some conditions in an abstract setting, it is automatically valid for any concrete setting where the conditions hold.

What are the conditions we'll hold ourselves to? Almost all of the laws for addition and multiplication that we know from ordinary real numbers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item There's an additive identity $0$ such that $x + 0 = x$ for all $x$.
    \item There's a multiplicative identity $1$ such that $x \cdot 1 = x$ for all $x$.
    \item Addition is associative:
        \begin{equation}
            \label{eq:addition_associativity}
            (x + y) + z = x + (y + z)
        \end{equation}
    \item Addition is commutative:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:addition_commutativity}
        x + y = y + x
    \end{equation}
    \item Multiplication is associative:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:multiplication_associativity}
        (x \cdot y) \cdot z = x \cdot (y \cdot z)
    \end{equation}
    \item Multiplication is distributive over addition from both sides:
    \begin{eqnarray}
        \label{eq:right_distributivity}
        x \cdot (y + z) &=& x \cdot y + x \cdot z   \\
        \label{eq:left_distributivity}
        (x + y) \cdot z &=& x \cdot z + y \cdot z
    \end{eqnarray}
\end{itemize}

Did you notice something missing from that list? We haven't assumed that multiplication is commutative\footnote{This is why we had to define two separate distributivity rules, one from the right \eqref{eq:right_distributivity} and one from the left \eqref{eq:left_distributivity}.}! In general we will assume we can't say that $x \cdot y$ is equal to $y \cdot x$ in general. Maybe sometimes it is for some particular numbers, but not for all possible pairs $(x, y)$. In fact, we will see that the failure of multiplication to commute is what makes the theory of exponentials so rich and interesting.

\subsection{Powers}

Like in the ordinary algebra you learned in school, we can multiply $x$ by itself many times to get powers:
\begin{eqnarray*}
    x \cdot x &=& x^2 \\
    x \cdot x \cdot x &=& x^3 \\
     &\vdots& \nonumber \\
    \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}} &=& x^n
\end{eqnarray*}
Note very carefully: $n$ here is not an object in our set $\mathcal{A}$. It's just an ordinary natural number that counts how many times we multiply $x$ by itself. Our exponents are (for now) just ordinary counting numbers.

And since we assumed above that multiplication is associative \eqref{eq:multiplication_associativity}, we can group those $n$ products in any way we like, so:
\begin{eqnarray}
    x^n \cdot x^m &=& \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}} \cdot \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{m \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    x^n \cdot x^m &=& \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{(n+m) \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    \label{eq:exponent_addition}
    x^n \cdot x^m &=& x^{n+m}
\end{eqnarray}

We can also ask: what if I power a power? Well, associativity is also helpful here:
\begin{eqnarray}
    (x^n)^m &=& \underbrace{(x^n) \cdot (x^n) \cdots (x^n)}_{m \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    (x^n)^m &=& \underbrace{(\underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}}) \cdot (\underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}}) \cdots (\underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}})}_{m \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    (x^n)^m &=& \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{(nm) \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    \label{eq:exponent_power}
    (x^n)^m &=& x^{nm}
\end{eqnarray}
Again: notice that $n$ and $m$ here are just ordinary counting numbers, so $nm$ is just ordinary multiplication of integers.

\subsection{Roots}

And also like in ordinary algebra, we can wonder about roots. We can ask: is there a $y \in \mathcal{A}$ such that $y^n = x$? There's no reason one has to exist. And even if it does, there's no reason why it has to be unique. But if such a $y$ exists, let's call it one of the $n$-th roots of $x$:
\begin{equation}
    y^n = x \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = \sqrt[n]{x}.
\end{equation}
But you could also think of equation \eqref{eq:exponent_power} and ask: isn't $x$ just $x^1$? So maybe we can write $y$ as some power of $x$ such that when raised to $n$ gives $x$. What power would that be? That is, what is $m$ such that:
\begin{equation}
    (x^m)^n = x^{mn} = x^1.
\end{equation}
Well, it's whatever $m$ such that $mn = 1$. Well, if you remember that here we're dealing with ordinary multiplication of integers, then $m = 1/n$ is the only option.

Notice what just happened: we quietly stepped outside our initial set of exponents. The exponent $1/n$ is not a counting number anymore, it's a fraction. The exponents are still not in $\mathcal{A}$. But we enlarged the values we allow for them, from integers to rationals. If we just think of it:
\begin{equation}
    x^{\frac{m}{n}} = x^{m \frac{1}{n}} = (x^m)^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{x^m}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The route that breaks, and the route that doesn't}

So we can follow the same recipe we used in $\mathbb{R}$, right up to this point. But now let's try to continue. Recall the steps we used to get exponential functions in $\mathbb{R}$:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \label{item:step_algebra} We had an algebra with an associative multiplication. \checkmark
    \item \label{item:step_rational} We raised elements to rational exponents using repeated multiplication and roots. \checkmark
    \item \label{item:step_real} We used limits of rational sequences to extend exponents to all of $\mathbb{R}$.
    \item \label{item:step_exp} We fixed the base and varied the exponent to get an exponential function.
\end{enumerate}

Steps \ref{item:step_algebra} and \ref{item:step_rational} went through just fine. But step \ref{item:step_real} runs into two problems in our abstract algebra $\mathcal{A}$.

The first problem is that our algebra has no notion of limits. To take $\lim_{n \to \infty} x^{r_n}$ we need to know what it means for a sequence of elements of $\mathcal{A}$ to converge. We haven't assumed anything like that. We could add it --- for matrices, there are natural notions of distance --- but we'd have to do that work explicitly.

The second problem is more fundamental. The whole argument in $\mathbb{R}$ relied on $x^{1/n}$ being a well-defined, unique object. For real numbers, the positive $n$-th root of a positive number is unique. For elements of $\mathcal{A}$, it isn't. We already flagged this: roots might not exist, and even if they do, there might be more than one. If $x^{1/2}$ is ambiguous, then $x^r$ defined as a limit of $x^{p_k/q_k}$ is ambiguous too. The whole construction falls apart.

So the route through roots and limits is closed to us. But remember the punchline from Section~\ref{sec:refresher}: the power series definition of the exponential
\begin{equation}
    e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}
\end{equation}
needed nothing beyond addition and multiplication. And we have those! So let's just use this directly.

For any element $X \in \mathcal{A}$, we \emph{define}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:exponential_power_series}
    e^X := \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{X^n}{n!} = 1 + X + \frac{X^2}{2!} + \frac{X^3}{3!} + \cdots
\end{equation}
where $X^n$ means $X$ multiplied by itself $n$ times (which we know how to do), and the coefficients $1/n!$ are just ordinary rational numbers (not elements of $\mathcal{A}$). As long as our algebra has some notion of convergence for infinite sums --- which for matrices it does --- this definition makes sense.

This is now our definition of the exponential in $\mathcal{A}$. It agrees with the ordinary exponential when $\mathcal{A} = \mathbb{R}$. And it works for matrices, operators, and anything else we can add and multiply. The question is: what properties does it have, and which familiar ones are we about to lose?

\chapter{What really is an exponential function?}

\section{Introduction}

\lettrine{Y}{ou} know exponentials, right? You started with powers and roots, and then eventually you extended them to real and complex numbers. And you did that through some really neat properties. You learned that when if you know how to calculate $e^x$ and $e^y$, you can calculate their product easily:
\begin{equation}
    e^{x}e^{y} = e^{x + y}.
\end{equation}
Simple rule. And if you remember your earliest brushes with that function, that was the property your calculus teacher used to define how we extend powers from rational to real numbers in the first place! And it works for complex numbers too.

Now imagine you're studying systems that evolve in time or describing rotations in space. These are no longer real or complex numbers. They're matrices, or more generally, operators. You'd like to use everything you know about exponentials to solve these problems. But here things break down. The familiar rules of exponentials no longer hold. For example, if $A$ and $B$ are matrices, it's generally not true that
\begin{equation}
    AB = BA
\end{equation}
So... what else breaks? These questions will lead us to discover elegant and useful structures. In this chapter, we'll build up the theory of exponentials from scratch, in a way that will make it work for more than numbers.

\section{The Exponential Function}

In the world of numbers, the exponential is usually defined as an extension of powers, or repeated multiplication from integer to non-integer exponents. So let's start in the same place.

Let's say we have some set of objects $\mathcal{A}= \{x, y, z, \ldots\}$. At this point we're not worried what they are. We'll could call them ``numbers'', but they could be more complicated objects as well. Let's say also that we know how to do two things with those objects: we can add them $x + y$ and we can multiply them $x \cdot y$.

Outside of those two operations, we'll keep the set $\mathcal{A}$ as abstract as we can. We could choose a specific set of concrete mathematical objects: matrices, functions in a certain domain, quaternions, \ldots, but we won't. And the reason is simple: whatever conclusions we reach by requiring just knowing how to add and multiply things will hold for any concrete set in which we know how to add and multiply things. That's the power of abstract math: if we prove that something holds given some conditions in an abstract setting, it is automatically valid for any concrete setting where the conditions hold.

What are the conditions we'll hold ourselves to? Almost all of the laws for addition and multiplication that we know from ordinary real numbers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item There's an additive identity $0$ such that $x + 0 = x$ for all $x$.
    \item There's a multiplicative identity $1$ such that $x \cdot 1 = x$ for all $x$.
    \item Addition is associative:
        \begin{equation}
            \label{eq:addition_associativity}
            (x + y) + z = x + (y + z)
        \end{equation}
    \item Addition is commutative:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:addition_commutativity}
        x + y = y + x
    \end{equation}
    \item Multiplication is associative:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:multiplication_associativity}
        (x \cdot y) \cdot z = x \cdot (y \cdot z)
    \end{equation}
    \item Multiplication is distributive over addition from both sides:
    \begin{eqnarray}
        \label{eq:right_distributivity}
        x \cdot (y + z) &=& x \cdot y + x \cdot z   \\
        \label{eq:left_distributivity}
        (x + y) \cdot z &=& x \cdot z + y \cdot z
    \end{eqnarray}
\end{itemize}

Did you notice something missing from that list? We haven't assumed that multiplication is commutative\footnote{This is why we had to define two separate distributivity rules, one from the right \eqref{eq:right_distributivity} and one from the left \eqref{eq:left_distributivity}.}! In general we will assume we can't say that $x \cdot y$ is equal to $y \cdot x$ in general. Maybe some times it is for some particular numbers, but not for all possible pairs $(x, y)$. In fact, we will see that the failure of multiplication to commute is what makes the theory of exponentials so rich and interesting.

\subsection{Powers}
Like in the ordinary algebra you learned in school, we can multiply $x$ by itself many times to get powers:
\begin{eqnarray*}
    x \cdot x &=& x^2 \\
    x \cdot x \cdot x &=& x^3 \\
     &\vdots& \nonumber \\
    \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}} &=& x^n
\end{eqnarray*}
Note very carefully: $n$ here is not an object in our set $\mathcal{A}$. It's just an ordinary natural number that counts how many times we multiply $x$ by itself. Our exponents are (for now) just ordinary counting numbers.

And since we assumed above that multiplication is associative \eqref{eq:multiplication_associativity}, we can group those $n$ products in any way we like, so:
\begin{eqnarray}
    x^n \cdot x^m &=& \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}} \cdot \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{m \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    x^n \cdot x^m &=& \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{(n+m) \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    \label{eq:exponent_addition}
    x^n \cdot x^m &=& x^{n+m}
\end{eqnarray}

We can also ask: what is I power a power? Well, associativity is also helpful here:
\begin{eqnarray}
    (x^n)^m &=& \underbrace{(x^n) \cdot (x^n) \cdots (x^n)}_{m \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    (x^n)^m &=& \underbrace{(\underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}}) \cdot (\underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}}) \cdots (\underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{n \text{ times}})}_{m \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    (x^n)^m &=& \underbrace{x \cdot x \cdots x}_{(nm) \text{ times}} \nonumber \\
    \label{eq:exponent_power}
    (x^n)^m &=& x^{nm}
\end{eqnarray}
Again: notice that $n$ and $m$ here are just ordinary counting numbers, so $nm$ is just ordinary multiplication of integers.
\subsection{Roots}
And also like in ordinary algebra, we can wonder about roots. We can ask: is there a $y \in \mathcal{A}$ such that $y^n = x$? There's no reason one has to exist. And even if it does, there's no reason why it has to be unique. But if such a y exists, let's call it one of the n-th roots of x:
\begin{equation}
    y^n = x \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = \sqrt[n]{x}.
\end{equation}
But you could also think of equation \eqref{eq:exponent_power} and ask: isn't $x$ just $x^1$? So maybe we can write $y$ as some power of $x$ such that when raised to $n$ gives $x$. What power would that be? That is, what is $m$ such that:
\begin{equation}
    (x^m)^n = x^{mn} = x^1.
\end{equation}
Well, it's whatever $m$ such that $mn = 1$. Well, if you remember that here we're dealing with ordinary multiplication of integers, then $m = 1/n$ is the only option.

Notice what just happened: we quietly stepped outside our initial set of exponents. The exponent $1/n$ is not a counting number anymore, it's a fraction. The exponents are still not in $\mathcal{A}$. But we enlarged the values we allow for them, from integers to rationals. If we just think of it:
\begin{equation}
    x^{\frac{m}{n}} = x^{m \frac{1}{n}} = (x^m)^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{x^m}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exponentials}

\subsubsection{A brief pit-stop in back  in $\mathbb{R}$}

Let's go back to the world of real numbers for a bit. Back in Calculus this would be the point where your teacher would do the following reasoning. If we can define $x^r$ for any rational number $r$, we can also define it if $r$ is a real number. Why? Cause we can define real numbers as limits of sequences of rational numbers. We can then extend the definition of $x^r$ to the case where $r$ is real by taking limits:
\begin{equation}
   \text{If } r = \lim_{n \to \infty} r_n \text{ with } r_n \in \mathbb{Q}, \text{ then } x^r := \lim_{n \to \infty} x^{r_n}
\end{equation}
So, if we fix a exponent $r \in \mathbb{R}$, we can define a function $\operatorname{pow}_r: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, that simply raises its argument to the $r$-th power:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{pow}_r(x)  =  x^r
\end{equation}

After that, the teacher would also ask: well, what if instead of fixing the exponent we fix the base? So, for each fixed base $r$, we can also define a function from reals to reals that raise that base to its arguments. We call those functions \emph{exponentials}. Formally, for each $r \in \mathbb{R}$ we can define a function $\exp_r: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$:
\begin{equation}
  \exp_r(x)  =  r^x
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Can we do the same on our general algebra $\mathcal{A}$?}

Ok, that was pretty neat. If feels like if we want to define exponential functions in our general algebra $\mathcal{A}$ we must follow the same instinct right? Let's recap the recipe we followed to define exponentials in $\mathbb{R}$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We defined an algebra in $\mathbb{R}$ with an associative multiplication.
  \item \label{item:rational_exponents} We defined a way to raise a number $x \in \mathbb{R}$ to a rational exponent $r \in \mathbb{Q}$, by using repeated multiplication and roots.
  \item \label{item:real_exponents}Use a notion of limit of rational sequences, to promote $r$ to a real number. So now all numbers in the expression  $x^y$ are \emph{in the same set}.
  \item \label{item:fix_base}This allows us to fix the base and vary the exponent to obtain a function $\exp_r: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ given by: $\exp_r(x) = r^x$
\end{enumerate}

Can we repeat the process in our non-commutative algebra $\mathcal{A}$? Well. Not really. We can go up to step $\ref{item:rational_exponents}$ as we did. If we endow our set $\mathcal{A}$ with some notion of limits we can even go to step \ref{item:real_exponents} and define how to raise a number $x\in\mathcal{A}$ to a real exponent $r\in\mathbb{R}$. But if we then fix the base and vary the exponent this will define a function from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathcal{A}$, not from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{A}$ like we want. We defined a way to raise numbers in $\mathcal{A}$ to the power of a real number, we want the oposite: how do we raise real numbers to the power of a number in $\mathcal{A}$?

Let's recall one important thing: our set $\mathcal{A}$ is an abstract algebra. We only know how to add and multiply numbers in this set, and we can only rely on that to define our exponential function. So our way out here is to go back to our definition of exponential functions for real numbers and ask ourselves: can it be defined in terms of only additions and multiplications? If we can, then we'll simply lift that definition to our abstract world and look what properties it carries.
